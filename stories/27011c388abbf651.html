Bukarti and DanBello: When is it haram to support a politician in Nigeria?, By Yushau A. Shuaib

2026-11-26

A commentary by Yushau A. Shuaib argues that Nigerians should be free to support candidates, join political parties or provide professional services to lawful campaigns without facing intimidation. It contends that political violence and efforts to damage people’s reputations—not political participation itself—are the greater threat to civic engagement.

The essay focuses on Bulama Bukarti and DanBello, two public figures whose possible involvement in politics has prompted criticism. Neither, Shuaib writes, had publicly responded to the allegations at the time of his article. He suggests that their failure to explain their positions before speculation grew left room for claims of betrayal. Shuaib says he understands Bukarti to be supporting a candidate he considers capable, and DanBello to be providing strategic communications and advocacy consultancy.

As a comparison, Shuaib points to Islamic scholar and public intellectual Ali Isa Pantami, who entered politics and joined a party he had previously criticised while in government. The essay says Pantami faced attacks from some fellow critics but defended his decision with reasoned arguments. Shuaib also argues that public and private political behaviour can differ, and that such differences do not by themselves establish dishonesty.

Shuaib condemns reports of the alleged stoning of President Tinubu’s convoy and attacks on praise-singer Kahutu Rarara, including in places of worship. He also discloses that he advised on a campaign opposing the Tinubu Must Go movement after being approached by Malam Kashifu Inuwa. He says he recommended the name Tinubu Movement for Sustainable Governance Foundation and urged factual messaging focused on peacebuilding and good governance; he says those recommendations were implemented with the tacit support of First Lady Oluremi Tinubu.

The author says he is making that disclosure to demonstrate the value of transparency in political engagement. His central argument is that opposition and support are both legitimate parts of democratic participation, while violence and coercion are unacceptable regardless of who uses them.
