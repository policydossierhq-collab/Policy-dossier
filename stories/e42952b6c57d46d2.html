Foreign governments are blocking japa, Nigeria must now give its talent a reason to stay

2026-10-27

Tighter immigration rules in several major destinations are narrowing options for Nigerian students and workers, even as some economic indicators at home improve. In Britain, the student visa refusal rate for Nigerians rose from 4.5% in the first half of 2025 to 22.7% in 2026. A lower threshold for universities’ sponsor refusal rates led some institutions to withdraw offers. Britain also raised the skills threshold for work visas and ended overseas recruitment of new care workers. The United States partially suspended immigrant visas for Nigerians from January 2026 and restricted several other visa categories, while later resuming processing for foreign-trained doctors. Canada has tightened parts of its system while creating targeted permanent-residence routes for some international doctors.

The changes are reflected in health-worker recruitment figures. In the year to March 2026, 1,614 Nigerian-trained nurses and midwives joined the UK register, down from 2,363 the previous year. Nigeria has 95,456 registered doctors, but 60,551 hold active practising licences; an estimate cited in the analysis puts the number working in the domestic health system at about 31,000. A 2025 national health report recorded 20,966 health professionals relocating abroad in one year, including 3,919 doctors and 7,487 nurses and midwives.

At the same time, some domestic indicators have improved. Inflation fell to 14.63% in September 2026, economic growth reached 4.43% in the second quarter, and personal transfers from abroad totalled $11.12 billion in the first half of the year. But electricity supply remains a major constraint: generation fell to 2,034 megawatts in an October collapse, despite installed capacity of 13,625 megawatts. The International Organization for Migration recorded 14,787 Nigerians returning with its support in 2025, though the figure does not distinguish voluntary returns from deportations or other forms of compelled return.

The analysis argues that fewer departures alone will not make return sustainable. It calls for more reliable electricity, improved security, stable economic conditions, recognition of overseas qualifications, pay that can compete for shortage occupations and better tracking of professionals. It also says people returning involuntarily should receive skills assessments and pathways into suitable work. Without such measures, the recent restrictions may reduce opportunities to leave without giving skilled Nigerians a lasting reason to build their futures at home.
