Africa begins rollout of cheaper HIV prevention injection

2026-11-26

Supplies of a lower-cost generic version of lenacapavir, a long-acting HIV prevention medicine, have begun arriving in Africa. The first global shipment reached Zambia, and Uganda is expected to receive supplies in the coming days, according to the Global Fund. Zimbabwe is due to receive supplies early next year, with other African countries scheduled to follow.

The injection is administered twice a year and is expected to cost about $40 per person annually. That is substantially less than the original version developed by Gilead Sciences, which reportedly cost about $28,000 a year in the United States. Lenacapavir targets HIV’s protective protein shell, disrupting the virus’s ability to multiply and establish infection.

In 2024 clinical trials involving more than 5,000 adolescent girls and young women in Uganda and South Africa, none of the participants who received the injection contracted HIV. The World Health Organization recommended lenacapavir for HIV prevention in July 2025. Unlike pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP, which generally involves taking medication daily, the injection may offer an alternative for people who find daily pills difficult or face stigma associated with using them.

The Global Fund says more than 189,000 people in 10 countries have already received their first lenacapavir injections. It expects 24 countries to receive supplies by the end of 2026 and, with support from the United States government, aims to reach at least three million people by the end of 2028. The medicine is eventually expected to be available in 120 low- and middle-income countries.

Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for nearly two-thirds of people living with HIV worldwide, and adolescent girls and young women are disproportionately affected. The rollout comes amid concerns that cuts to US foreign assistance could affect HIV programmes in countries reliant on international funding. The United Nations has set a goal of ending AIDS as a public health threat by 2030.
