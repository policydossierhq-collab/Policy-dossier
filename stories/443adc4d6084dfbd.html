Six climate measures could save 1.6 million lives yearly – WHO

2026-11-04

Six climate and health interventions could prevent an estimated 1.6 million deaths each year and avoid 16.7 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions annually, according to a World Health Organisation report. The projected emissions reduction is equivalent to 31 per cent of annual global greenhouse gas emissions, excluding emissions from land use and forestry.

The measures are heat-health warning systems; climate-resilient drinking-water services; basic sanitation; hygiene and handwashing facilities; cleaner household fuels and technologies for cooking and heating; and more efficient fossil fuel pricing policies. The WHO says the interventions could produce $3.9 trillion in annual economic welfare benefits and $5.4 trillion in fiscal gains if implemented globally.

The report estimates that five of the interventions could return $24 in benefits for every dollar invested, with higher returns expected in low- and middle-income countries. Heat-health warning systems are projected to deliver particularly large returns—an estimated $835 for every dollar invested—because expanding existing systems is relatively inexpensive and could help prevent heat-related illness.

Efficient fossil fuel pricing policies are projected to generate the largest overall gains. Measures such as removing inefficient fossil fuel subsidies and appropriately taxing fossil fuels could prevent 1.2 million deaths a year, while producing $3 trillion in economic welfare benefits and $5.4 trillion in fiscal gains, the report says.

The WHO described the report as its first comprehensive assessment of the economic and fiscal returns from scaling up the six interventions. It argues that many health effects of climate change can be addressed with existing solutions, but governments need to put them into practice across health and other sectors. WHO Director-General Tedros Ghebreyesus urged governments to put health at the centre of climate action, particularly as countries prepare their commitments for the next round of international climate negotiations.
