“Ba A Gudu Ba A Bar Gida”: Fifth Chukker, photography, cultural memory, and the responsibility of belonging, By Mudi Yahaya

2026-11-05

“Ba A Gudu Ba A Bar Gida,” an archival photography exhibition at the Fifth Chukker Polo and Country Club Resort in Kangimi, Kaduna, uses images by Nina Fischer-Stephan to explore belonging, historical memory and the responsibility to preserve cultural knowledge. Its Hausa title is understood as “One does not flee and abandon one’s home.” The exhibition’s framing treats home as more than buildings and familiar landscapes: it also depends on the knowledge through which people understand their relationships and imagine a shared future.

Fischer-Stephan was born in Munich in 1922 and moved to Ibadan in 1959 with her husband, Klaus Stephan, then an Africa correspondent for German television. For more than eleven years, his press passes afforded her access to Northern Nigeria’s royal courts, prominent figures and architectural heritage. The photographs document the region around the early years of independence, inviting viewers to consider how political change was experienced in everyday life—in homes, streets, clothing, work and encounters.

The exhibition presents the archive as a starting point for inquiry, not a complete account of the society it depicts. Photographs preserve traces of social life, but their meanings depend on context, research and engagement with people connected to the places and subjects shown. A building, market or garment may prompt questions about social organisation and local knowledge; appearance alone cannot settle those questions. The images should also be considered alongside Nigerian photographers and local practices of image-making, rather than treated as the beginning of Northern Nigeria’s photographic history.

The exhibition’s argument carries particular relevance amid insecurity, displacement, economic hardship and political uncertainty in Northern Nigeria ahead of the 2027 general elections. Its title is not presented as a judgement on people who leave for safety: belonging can persist through departure, memory and community ties. In Kaduna, the photographs can bring archival records into conversation with living knowledge, while recognising that memories may differ and require careful corroboration. The exhibition’s call is to preserve evidence, question inherited interpretations and leave future generations the means to understand the past more fully—without nostalgia or claims that exceed what the archive can support.
