Masussuka’s killing highlights Nigeria’s extremism, terrorism problem

2026-11-01

Islamic cleric Yahaya Masussuka was shot dead outside his home in Katsina State on the evening of 26 October. His friend, Adamu Abubakar, was also killed. Police said two people on a motorcycle carried out the attack. Ansaru, a breakaway faction of Boko Haram, later claimed responsibility, saying it targeted Masussuka over his rejection of hadiths and support for peaceful coexistence among people of different faiths. The claim has not been independently established in the supplied material.

Some of Masussuka’s supporters initially blamed scholars from rival religious movements, citing earlier threats and online disputes. But there is no established evidence connecting those groups or the Izala movement to the killing. Some Izala scholars have rejected the accusations. Masussuka’s teachings, described as “Quran-only,” drew opposition, though he said he rejected only hadiths he believed contradicted the Quran or justified violence. Amnesty International Nigeria condemned the killing and called for an investigation; President Bola Tinubu also condemned it and directed security agencies to investigate.

The reaction to the killing has drawn attention to how theological disputes can become a source of hostility. In research on Muslim identities and conflict in northern Nigeria, the late academic Abdul Mustapha examined how rival claims to religious authenticity have intensified divisions and, at times, violence. The practice of declaring another Muslim an unbeliever, known as takfirism, has also been used by violent Sunni jihadists to portray religious opponents as illegitimate. Ibrahim Makari, an imam at the National Mosque in Abuja, warned that declaring someone an unbeliever can be a “gate to assassination.”

Religious intimidation is not confined to Muslim communities or northern Nigeria. In 2022, Christian student Deborah Samuel was killed by fellow students after being accused of blasphemy, and Christian Rhoda Jatau was later arrested and charged after sharing a message condemning the killing. Traditional religious practitioners have also reported threats and discrimination. In Delta State, a court ruled that the harassment of traditional worshippers was unconstitutional after a shrine and artefacts were destroyed in 2024.

Auwal Rafsanjani of the Civil Society Legislative Advocacy Centre said extremist ideas that promote hatred and justify violence can create a path to terrorism, while noting that not all extremists are terrorists. He called for religious and traditional leaders to counter extremist narratives and protect young people from recruitment. He also said governments should distinguish criminal violence from peaceful religious belief and lawful dissent, and not use counterterrorism as a pretext for persecution or restrictions on fundamental freedoms.
